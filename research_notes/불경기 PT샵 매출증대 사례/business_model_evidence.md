# Cross-country evidence: PT business models and operating policies that hold up in downturns

Method note: These notes come from about 19 web searches run on 2026-10-19. Every WebFetch call failed with a DNS error (ENOTFOUND for healthandfitness.org, ideafit.com and asiae.co.kr), so the figures below come from search-result extracts of the cited pages. I could not open the full pages. The report writer should treat each number as "as reported by the cited page" and, where possible, spot-check the high-stakes ones (HFA 66.4% retention, TRP percentages, IDEA 2009 figures). Source-quality flags: [ASSOC] = trade association (self-interested in a positive industry story, often voluntary and unrandomized samples), [ACAD] = peer-reviewed, [VENDOR] = software vendor or consultant with a commercial interest (weak), [PRESS] = trade or general press, [CONSULT-RESEARCH] = consultancy research reported in trade press, not peer-reviewed.

## 1. Fitness spending in recessions: is fitness resilient, and which segments gain or lose?

### Takeaway
In 2008–09 and in the 2022–24 cost-of-living squeeze, health clubs were relatively resilient. Revenue was flat to slightly up and membership held, and in household budgets gyms were usually cut after eating out. Dues revenue did erode for a sizeable minority of clubs, though, and consumers were highly sensitive to price rises. Within personal training, the clearest recession-era shift was from 1:1 sessions toward shared (2-person and 3–5-person) sessions. After the downturns, the industry grew strongly: in the HFA's 2025 report, revenue rose about 8% and membership about 6% in 2024.

### Cited Findings
**2008–2009**
- IHRSA's 2009 Global Report put 2008 worldwide health-club revenue at about $68.2B with about 117.5M members, and described North America as "setting the pace" on resiliency. [ASSOC] — [fit-pro.com summary of IHRSA 2009 Global Report](https://fit-pro.com/article-1344-IHRSA-Releases-2009-IHRSA-Global-Report-The-State-of-the-Health-Club-Industry.html); [Australasian Leisure Management](https://www.ausleisure.com.au/news/ihrsas-asia-pacific-convention-success)
- A February 2009 IHRSA survey of 269 mostly single-club operators found that at least two-thirds reported January 2009 total revenue, membership and new sales equal to or better than January 2008. Clubs cut staffing and expenses and delayed equipment purchases. [ASSOC, voluntary sample] — [American Spa](https://www.americanspa.com/commercial-clubs/survey-indicates-ihrsa-clubs-performed-well-january-2009); [SGB](https://sgbonline.com/?p=26908)
- By August 2009, 42% of sampled clubs reported dues revenue down more than 5% year over year, while 52% reported steady or higher non-dues revenue (PT, programs and similar). [ASSOC via PRESS] — [Health Club Management](https://www.healthclubmanagement.co.uk/health-club-management-news/IHRSA-predicts-profitable-autumn-results/157983)
- A more critical industry commentary called the sector "flat" in 2009, though it still did better than manufacturing. It cited about a 2% revenue increase among member clubs in 2009 after a weak 2008. [PRESS] — [Athletic Business](https://www.athleticbusiness.com/home/article/15142967/club-performance-climbing-or-on-a-treadmill)
- US membership was flat at about 41.5M in January 2008. [PRESS] — [San Diego Business Journal](https://www.sdbj.com/?p=13945)
- In IDEA's 2009 trends survey (US trainers), the share of respondents offering 2-person shared sessions rose 10 percentage points and 3–5-person sessions rose 18 points. IDEA attributed this to consumers cutting costs during the recession and to trainers keeping clients at a lower per-person fee while earning more per hour. [ASSOC/trade self-report] — [American Spa: IDEA research](https://www.americanspa.com/market-research-reveals-top-trends-personal-training)
- In the same survey, 58% of respondents offered small-group training (3–5 clients), up 9 points year over year, and 46% expected the category to grow. Boot camps (47%) and small-group circuit training (55%) were tracked for the first time in 2009. Offering of 3–5-person sessions rose from 34% in 2001 to 60% in 2009. — [IDEA: Personal Trainers Meet the Needs of Savvy, Cost-Conscious Consumers](https://www.ideafit.com/personal-trainers-meet-the-needs-of-savvy-cost-conscious-consumers-0); [fit-pro.com on IDEA 14th Annual Report](https://fit-pro.com/article-1424-IDEA-14th-Annual-Programs-Equipment-Trends-Report-Results.html)

**2022–2025 (inflation and cost of living)**
- The 2025 HFA Global Report, covering about 30 countries with 2024 data, found that among countries with comparable year-over-year data, revenue grew an average of 8%, membership 6% and facilities nearly 4%. 91% of business leaders expected revenue growth in 2025 and 83% expected profitability gains. [ASSOC] — [HFA: The 2025 HFA Global Report](https://www.healthandfitness.org/the-2025-hfa-global-report/); [RecManagement](https://recmanagement.com/articles/155339/fitness-industrys-global-momentum-continued-2024-new-report-shows)
- In a Hussle survey of about 2,900 UK gym-goers (January 2023), 57% said they would reduce gym visits if prices rose. In an earlier Hussle survey (late 2022), 22% would cut eating out first versus 18% who would cut the gym first. [VENDOR, a gym-pass aggregator] — [Hussle](https://www.hussle.com/blog/cost-of-living-overtakes-covid-as-biggest-threat-to-gym-usage)
- In a Total Fitness (UK operator) survey of 11,000 members and ex-members, almost half considered their membership essential and would cut other spending first. The operator reported no significant cost-of-living hit to membership numbers. [operator self-report] — [Health Club Management, "The cost-of-living crisis"](https://www.healthclubmanagement.co.uk/health-club-management-features/The-cost-of-living-crisis/35957)
- In a CBRE/Censuswide UK survey (February 2024), 73% planned to increase or maintain gym-visit frequency despite financial worries. CBRE describes gyms as "leading leisure". [PRESS/real-estate consultancy] — [CBRE](https://www.cbre.co.uk/insights/articles/why-gyms-are-leading-leisure)
- In PureGym's UK survey, 54% of non-members said they would not join a gym that year because it is too expensive. This points to price as the entry barrier for new customers. [operator] — reported in [Health Club Management / FitTech cost-of-living feature](https://www.fittechglobal.com/fit-tech-features/Talking-point-The-cost-of-living-crisis/35957)
- McKinsey's Future of Wellness 2025 surveyed more than 9,000 consumers in China, Germany, the UK and the US. 84% rank health and wellness as a top priority. Gen Z accounts for about 41% of annual US wellness spend while making up about a third of US adults. Coverage sizes the wellness market at about $2T, but other coverage gives $1.5T and $1.8T from different editions. [CONSULT-RESEARCH via PRESS] — [European Spa Magazine](https://europeanspamagazine.com/mckinsey-reveals-the-future-of-wellness-report-2025/); [McKinsey explainer](https://www.mckinsey.com/featured-insights/mckinsey-explainers/what-is-the-future-of-wellness); [WEF](https://www.weforum.org/stories/health-and-healthcare-systems/wellness-market-mental-health-physical-mckinsey-consumers-retail-lifestyle/)

### Inferences
- The mechanism behind recession resilience, in both 2009 and 2022–24, was that existing members held on while price sensitivity rose. That makes keeping current clients, and offering a cheaper way in, more important than new-member acquisition at full price.
- In 2009, non-dues revenue (which includes PT) held up better than dues for about half of clubs, and the move to shared PT formats was explicitly framed as a way to keep cost-cutting clients. Small-group or semi-private PT is the documented recession-adaptation pattern.
- Resilience evidence comes mostly from trade-association and operator surveys, which have an incentive to report good news. It is directionally consistent across sources but not rigorous.

### Gaps
- I found no academic or government household-expenditure analysis isolating gym or PT spending elasticity in 2008–09. Candidate sources not retrieved: BLS Consumer Expenditure Survey or ONS "sports and recreation services" series.
- The Les Mills Global Fitness Report 2026 has a section on what consumers are "up against", but I could not read the full report and found no cost-of-living figures from it. — [Les Mills 2026 report page](https://www.lesmills.com.au/clubs-and-facilities/insight-hub/2026-global-fitness-report)
- I found no fitness-specific spending figure in McKinsey 2025 coverage, and no Mindbody/ClassPass or ABC Fitness data on 2008–09 or 2022–25 spending within the call budget.
- I found no PT-specific (as opposed to club-level) revenue data for either downturn.

## 2. Unit economics: 1:1 PT vs semi-private and small-group PT

### Takeaway
No rigorous published benchmark exists. Trade and consultant sources agree that semi-private (2–4) and small-group (4–6) formats lower each client's price to roughly 30–70% of the 1:1 rate while raising trainer revenue per hour to roughly 2–3x. That holds only when sessions are full: at low fill, 1:1 can earn more.

### Cited Findings
- Two-Brain Business's rule of thumb is to double the 1:1 hourly rate and divide by three, so each semi-private client pays about two-thirds of the 1:1 price (for example, $100/hr 1:1 becomes $66.66 per client). They report gyms can gross $200–250/hr with four clients. [VENDOR/consultant] — [Two-Brain Business: Semi-Private vs Small-Group](https://twobrainbusiness.com/semi-private-vs-small-group-training/); [Two-Brain: data-backed pricing](https://twobrainbusiness.com/200-per-session/)
- Gymkee (English page) claims semi-private earns a trainer $120–180/hr versus $60–80/hr for 1:1. Its Hindi page gives a 3–4.5x multiplier instead, so the two pages are inconsistent. [VENDOR, weak] — [Gymkee](https://gymkee.com/uk/blog/semi-private-personal-training-pricing-uk)
- GymInsight says per-client semi-private pricing is typically "just under" the 1:1 hourly rate. [VENDOR] — [GymInsight blog](https://blog.gyminsight.com/?p=13518)
- In a worked break-even example, a coach charging $65 for 1:1 would need about 10 attendees in a lower-priced group class to beat that hourly rate. Below that, 1:1 earns more. [blog] — [Goodreads author blog: "Are your group classes costing you money?"](https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/23786943-are-your-group-classes-costing-you-money-calculations-and-solutions)
- PushPress cites 4–6 clients as the usual small-group sweet spot. [VENDOR] — [PushPress](https://www.pushpress.com/blog/what-is-small-group-personal-training)
- IDEA's 2009 framing was that shared sessions let trainers earn more per hour while each client pays less. [ASSOC] — [American Spa: IDEA research](https://www.americanspa.com/market-research-reveals-top-trends-personal-training)
- IDEA Fit's "The profits of personal training in small groups" article exists, but I could not fetch it. — [IDEA Fit](https://www.ideafit.com/fitness-library/the-profits-of-personal-training-in-small-groups)

### Inferences
- A typical pattern is about 3 clients at about 60–67% of the 1:1 price, which gives about 1.8–2x the 1:1 hourly revenue. That lowers each client's cost by a third, which matters in a downturn, while raising trainer yield.
- The main operating risk is fill rate. Fixed schedules, small capacity caps (3–6) and a waitlist or booking discipline determine whether the economics work.
- Retention in small groups plausibly benefits from the social effects described in Section 4. This is inference; I found no direct PT-format retention comparison.

### Gaps
- I found no Club Industry or HFA profitability benchmark giving PT revenue per trainer hour by format, and no controlled data on client retention or price elasticity for semi-private versus 1:1.

## 3. Recurring membership vs prepaid package models (churn, cash flow)

### Takeaway
I found no independent or controlled data comparing these models. Vendor and consultant sources agree that recurring billing gives steadier, more forecastable revenue, while packs front-load cash but cause seasonal swings and a "renewal cliff" when the pack runs out. Industry-wide club retention is about 66–67% at 12 months, and early months are the main risk period.

### Cited Findings
- The HFA 2025 Fitness Industry Benchmarking Report (175 companies, more than 17,000 facilities, 27 countries) puts average member retention at 66.4%, meaning about a third cancel each year. The widely quoted 71.4% figure traces to mid-2010s IHRSA data. [ASSOC, via secondary blog] — [Uptivo summary](https://uptivo.fit/blog/gym-member-retention-statistics)
- IHRSA's "One Million Strong" study (2012–15, North America) found 67.2% of members stay at least 12 months but only 44.4% stay at least 24 months. Independent-club members were 47% more likely to cancel than chain members after controlling for age, gender and payment type. [ASSOC] — [Health Club Management: One million strong](https://www.healthclubmanagement.co.uk/health-club-management-features/One-million-strong/30115)
- IHRSA's 2018 Health Club Consumer Report found that higher usage frequency goes with higher retention. [ASSOC] — [HFA](https://www.healthandfitness.org/?p=722)
- Vendor sources say subscriptions smooth cash flow, while packs cause seasonal swings (for example, many 10-packs sold in January and none in February) but bring in cash up front. They also warn against deep pack discounts, which turn the pack price into the ceiling of what the trainer's time is worth. [VENDOR] — [Gymkee: session packs vs subscriptions](https://gymkee.com/blog/personal-training-session-packs-vs-subscriptions/); [Coachway](https://coachway.io/articles/personal-training-packages/)
- One blog claims subscriptions improve retention by 28–40% versus pack models, attributed to IHRSA. I could not find the original IHRSA source, so treat it as unverified. Another claims 44% of subscription cancellations happen in the first 90 days. [VENDOR, unverified] — [Gymkee (Hindi page)](https://gymkee.com/hi/blog/personal-training-session-packs-vs-subscriptions-hi); [Fitune](https://www.fitune.io/post/switching-to-subscription-based-personal-training-the-benefits-case-studies-and-how-to-do-it)

### Inferences
- In a downturn, recurring monthly billing with fewer sessions per month and no large lump sum lowers the client's commitment hurdle and the trust hurdle covered in Section 6. Large prepaid packs maximize short-term cash but carry renewal-cliff and trust risk.
- A hybrid of a recurring base plus a small pack as an entry point is the consensus in practitioner sources, though it is not empirically validated.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed or association data directly comparing churn or lifetime value between PT subscriptions and prepaid packs.
- I found no separate retention rate for PT clients versus general members.

## 4. Retention and referral economics (acquisition vs retention cost, churn, coach contact)

### Takeaway
About a third of members leave each year, and new members drop out fastest. The strongest quantitative levers are staff and coach interaction and social ties. In the UK TRP research, the right kind of staff interaction was associated with 45–86% lower cancellation risk, and making a friend at the gym with about 40% lower risk. Recommendation is the dominant acquisition channel, with about 59% of people choosing a workout venue on a friend's recommendation in Mindbody's 2018 data.

### Cited Findings
**Churn and early dropout (academic)**
- Gjestvang et al. (BMJ Open, 2019) followed new members (125 recruited from 25 Oslo fitness clubs, within 4 weeks of joining) for 12 months, classifying them as regular attendees, low attendees and dropouts. At 3 months, regular attendees had higher VO2max than dropouts, and VO2max was the only factor associated with attendance. 92.8% joined mainly to improve physical fitness. [ACAD] — [BMJ Open](https://bmjopen.bmj.com/content/9/4/e027987)
- The same research group reports that only 37% of new members exercised regularly, and only 17% used the gym twice weekly, through the first year. Lack of time and lack of energy were the most common dropout reasons. [ACAD group, via a paid promotional article by the Norwegian School of Sport Sciences; verify against the papers] — [ScienceNorway partner article](https://partner.sciencenorway.no/exercise-fitness-health/why-do-so-many-people-pay-for-unused-gym-memberships/1983338)
- Follow-up papers examined psychosocial predictors of regular exercise in year one (self-efficacy, motives, social support, life satisfaction, customer satisfaction) and whether repeated physical testing increases attendance. [ACAD] — [Frontiers in Psychology 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.638928/pdf); [JSSM 2021](https://jssm.org/20-1-35.p_d_f)

**Staff and coach contact (UK TRP research, Dr Melvyn Hillsdon)**
- In TRP's study of about 10,000 members, newer, younger and infrequent members most often reported never being spoken to by reception, and were the most at risk of cancelling. Had reception spoken to all members at least occasionally, cancellations would have been an estimated 30% lower over the 7-month follow-up. [CONSULT-RESEARCH] — [Health Club Management: "Does it matter what I say?"](https://www.healthclubmanagement.co.uk/health-club-management-features/‘Does-it-matter-what-I-say’/30910)
- Members whom fitness staff do not talk to have about a 3% monthly cancellation risk, and because so many members are in that group, universal contact has a large aggregate effect. [CONSULT-RESEARCH] — [Health Club Management: Warm welcome, fond farewell](https://www.healthclubmanagement.co.uk/health-club-management-features/Warm-welcome-fond-farewell/30127)
- Members making a "high-risk" visit have about a 5% chance of cancelling the next month. 48% of high-risk visits involved no interaction. One interaction type was associated with 86% lower next-month cancellation risk and another with 55% lower, and the effective types secured a return visit within 7 days or on a committed date. [CONSULT-RESEARCH] — [FitTech: Retention, does it matter what I say?](https://www.fittechglobal.com/fit-tech-features/Retention-Does-it-matter-what-I-say/30910)
- In an IHRSA/TRP report (more than 13,000 UK members), inactive members who were successfully re-engaged into revisiting were 45% less likely to cancel than those with no interaction. [ASSOC/CONSULT] — [Leisure Opportunities](https://leisureopportunities.co.uk/news/Fitness-retention-IHRSA/325741)

**Social ties and referrals**
- In the TRP 10,000-member data (2013), members who made a friend at the gym in the previous 3 months were about 40% less likely to cancel. This is correlational. [CONSULT-RESEARCH] — [Health Club Management: What do 10,000 members say?](https://www.healthclubmanagement.co.uk/health-club-management-features/What-do-10000-members-say/29553)
- Members who use only gym equipment were reported as 56% more likely to cancel than those who exercise in groups. This is attributed to IHRSA but I could not verify the original. [PRESS, unverified] — [Athletic Business](https://www.athleticbusiness.com/facilities/fitness/article/15151072/social-environments-help-health-clubs-with-member-retention)
- In Mindbody's 2018 data, 58.9% of people looking for a place to work out chose it on a friend's recommendation, and 50% of regular exercisers say they are more motivated with a workout buddy. Mindbody recommends refer-a-friend schemes and group workouts for retention. [VENDOR] — [Health Club Management: Social fitness key to boosting revenue](https://www.healthclubmanagement.co.uk/health-club-management-news/Social-fitness-key-to-boosting-revenue-–-Mindbody-report/338907)
- A 2021 Les Mills report found two-thirds of gym members prefer working out with others. [VENDOR/program provider] — [Les Mills: Live revival](https://www.lesmills.com/fit-planet/media/live-revival-sees-fitness-fans-flock-back-to-facilities)
- A claim that referred members stay 25–30% longer than those acquired through digital ads is unverified marketing-blog content. [VENDOR, weak] — [Recess.tv](https://www.recess.tv/blog-posts/boutique-fitness-marketing-7-strategies-that-actually-fill-your-classes)

### Inferences
- For a PT shop, structured coach contact (check-ins, booking the next session before the client leaves, follow-up after a missed session) has the best-quantified retention link in the evidence I found. "Commit to the next visit" interactions were the effective type.
- Social formats (small group, buddy or partner pricing) and referral incentives use the same mechanism: friendship lowers churn and recommendation drives acquisition. That fits a recession, when paid acquisition is less efficient.
- The first 3 months are the dropout window (Gjestvang). Onboarding and early fitness wins, such as measurable VO2max or strength gains and repeat testing, are plausible levers.

### Gaps
- HFA's "The Cost of Replacing a Health Club Member" article could not be fetched (DNS error), so I have no verified acquisition-versus-retention cost multiple. Do not use the generic "5x cheaper to retain" claim without a source. — [HFA article (not retrieved)](https://healthandfitness.org/improve-your-club/the-cost-of-replacing-a-health-club-member)
- I found no controlled study of referred versus non-referred member lifetime value.

## 5. Low-barrier entry offers (paid assessments, trials, challenges) and conversion

### Takeaway
Only vendor or consultant data exists, and it conflicts. The recurring claim is that free trials convert at under 20%, while structured paid intro offers (about 10–21 days, or 3–4 sessions) convert at 30% or more. One vendor gives 30–50% trial-to-member conversion for gyms. There is indirect academic support for fitness testing: in Gjestvang's research, fitness gains tracked attendance, and the group studied repeated testing.

### Cited Findings
- FitDegree says studios relying on free trials often see conversion below 20%, while structured paid intro offers reach 30% or more conversion to a second purchase. It recommends 10–21-day paid intro packages, with 14 days most common. [VENDOR] — [FitDegree](https://fitdegree.com/post/should-you-offer-the-first-class-free-how-to-handle-this-question-without-devaluing-your-studio)
- Gleantap gives a gym and fitness trial-to-member conversion benchmark of 30–50%, with no methodology disclosed. [VENDOR] — [Gleantap glossary](https://gleantap.com/glossary/trial-conversion-rate/)
- Vibefam argues that a nominal paid trial qualifies leads better than a free trial, but gives no numbers. [VENDOR] — [Vibefam](https://vibefam.com/what-studio-owners-get-wrong-about-free-trials-and-what-converts-better/)
- In an anecdotal UK studio example, a 3–4-class intro pack needed only 10–15 trialists to match the revenue from 70–90 single-class trialists. [newsletter anecdote] — [Wellness Wealth Weekly](https://wellnesswealthweekly.beehiiv.com/p/looking-to-improve-your-conversion-rate)
- The fitness and nutrition landing-page conversion average is 13.2% (Apexure 2026). This is a different metric from trial conversion. [VENDOR] — [Swipe Pages](https://swipepages.com/create/fitness-landing-page)
- Gjestvang's group studied whether repeated physical testing increases attendance in new members. [ACAD] — [JSSM 2021](https://jssm.org/20-1-35.p_d_f)

### Inferences
- A low-price paid assessment or short paid intro block, such as 2–4 weeks or 3–4 sessions, is the practitioner-preferred entry offer over free trials. In a downturn it also meets the price-barrier finding (54% citing cost as the reason not to join) without discounting the core product.
- Challenges with measurable before-and-after testing line up with the academic finding that early fitness improvement goes with continued attendance.

### Gaps
- I found no independent or academic dataset on intro-offer or challenge conversion rates, and no data specific to PT assessment-to-package conversion.

## 6. Consumer trust in prepaid PT packages; effect of transparency and refund guarantees

### Takeaway
Losses on prepaid packages when a gym closes are a documented, recurring consumer-protection problem in several countries (US, Singapore, Hong Kong, Australia, Korea). Korea saw a record 577 gym closures in 2025, with reports of operators selling prepaid PT and then disappearing. I found no quantitative study of how refund guarantees or price transparency affect PT conversion.

### Cited Findings
- Korea: a record 577 gyms closed in 2025, the most since records began, with concerns over prepaid membership losses and cases of operators selling prepaid PT packages and then disappearing (September 2026). [PRESS] — [Asia Economy](https://view.asiae.co.kr/en/article/2026092409460956711)
- Western Australia: Consumer Protection investigated complaints after a Perth Hills gym closed, leaving customers unable to recover prepaid memberships and personal-training sessions. [PRESS] — [Australasian Leisure Management](https://www.ausleisure.com.au/news/consumer-protection-investigates-complaints-following-closure-of-perth-hills-gym)
- Singapore: California Fitness's liquidation left prepaid customers without remedies, and the consumer association CASE received 331 complaints in four days. CASE advised credit-card chargeback requests for undelivered services. [law firm] — [Morgan Lewis](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/one-of-the-largest-member-clubs-in-singapore-is-liquidated)
- Hong Kong: the Consumer Council advises keeping contracts and receipts and contacting the card issuer quickly after lump-sum card payments to fitness centres that shut down. [government consumer body] — [Consumer Council HK](https://consumer.org.hk/en/shopping-guide/health-and-beauty/2024-tips-fitness-centre-shut-down)
- US (Washington State): a PT client prepaid $3,600, attended 3 sessions, and the club then closed. Her attorney sought class-action status. [PRESS] — [HeraldNet](https://www.heraldnet.com/?p=302336)
- US (South Carolina): the Department of Consumer Affairs advises seeking a refund of the unearned prepaid amount, then filing a complaint. [government] — [SC Consumer Affairs PDF](https://www.consumer.sc.gov/sites/consumer/files/Documents/News/2015/15004.pdf)
- Canada (Toronto): Track Fitness closed abruptly in 2019, affecting prepaid clients. [PRESS] — [blogTO](https://www.blogto.com/sports_play/2019/07/track-fitness-toronto-closes/)

### Inferences
- Large lump-sum PT prepayment carries a well-publicized default risk, and the risk is higher in downturns, when closures rise. Shops that cut the client's exposure through monthly billing, smaller session blocks, pro-rata refund terms in writing, or card or escrow-style payment can plausibly use that as a trust differentiator. This is inference; I found no conversion study measuring it.
- Together with Section 3, this points toward recurring or small-block billing in a downturn, for both cash-flow stability and trust.

### Gaps
- I found no experimental or survey data on how money-back guarantees, refund transparency or contract terms change PT purchase conversion. Possible avenues: national consumer-agency complaint statistics (for example, the Korea Consumer Agency's fitness complaint counts and refund-dispute shares) and general retail research on money-back guarantees. None were retrieved here.
